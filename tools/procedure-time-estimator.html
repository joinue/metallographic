Total Procedure Time Estimator

Estimate the total time required for complete metallographic sample preparation, including grinding, polishing, mounting, etching, and cleaning steps. Use this to plan your workflow and schedule sample preparation work.

In Manual mode, batch size multiplies grinding & polishing time. In Automated mode, samples run in parallel on a holder.
Reference & Background
Workflow

The metallographic preparation pipeline

A complete prep cycle moves through a fixed sequence of stages. The total time you spend depends on which stages you include, how aggressive the starting damage is, and whether you're prepping samples one at a time or in batches on an automated holder.

1

Section & mount

Cut the specimen, then encapsulate it in resin so it can be handled. Mounting is optional for large or rigid parts.

2

Grind

Sequence through coarse-to-fine SiC papers (or diamond disks) to remove sectioning damage and produce a flat, planar surface.

3

Polish

Diamond and oxide suspensions on cloth pads, stepping down through 9 → 6 → 3 → 1 → 0.25 → 0.05 μm to a scratch-free finish.

4

Clean

Rinse between each step (water + alcohol) and finish with an ultrasonic clean to remove embedded abrasive.

5

Etch

Chemical or electrolytic etch to reveal grain boundaries, phases, and microstructural features for microscopy.

6

Examine

Image under a metallurgical or SEM. If features aren't crisp, return to polishing or re-etch. Never skip back to grinding without protecting the polish.

Workflow choice

Manual vs. automated prep

Manual One at a time

  • Throughput: Linear with batch size. Twice the samples, twice the time.
  • Best for: Low-volume labs, one-off samples, training, awkward geometries.
  • Control: Full operator control over pressure and time per step.
  • Variability: Higher operator-to-operator variation.

Automated Parallel batch

  • Throughput: Per-batch time is the same for 1 or 6 samples on a single holder.
  • Best for: Routine QA, production labs, reproducibility-critical work (round-robin testing).
  • Control: Programmed force, RPM, time, all repeatable.
  • Variability: Low; results comparable across operators and shifts.

Practice

Tips for realistic estimates

  • Front-load the mounting cure. Castable epoxy cures for hours, so start at end of day. The mount will be ready when you walk in. Schedule lab time accordingly.
  • Batch by material. An automated holder of 6 same-material samples runs through one recipe; mixing hard and soft alloys forces a compromise that's worse for both.
  • Use the right starting grit. A clean band-saw cut often skips 120/240 and starts at 320, saving ~2 min per sample with no quality penalty. Heavy sectioning damage usually needs 120.
  • Don't skip more than one polishing step. Each tier removes scratches roughly 3× finer than the previous. Skipping two leaves residual damage that the next step can't reach in a reasonable time.
  • Watch the "easy" steps. Cleaning and etching look trivial on paper but add up across a day: 30 s × 8 steps × 6 samples = 24 minutes.
  • Plan for re-prep. Roughly 10–20% of samples in real labs need a re-polish after first inspection. Budget for it.
Common questions

Frequently asked questions

Why is automated faster only above one sample?

An automated holder runs all samples in parallel under the same recipe, so total time is the same for 1 or 6 samples. Manual prep is strictly sequential, so each extra sample adds another grinding-plus-polishing cycle. The crossover is usually 2–3 samples.

Are the polishing times the same for every material?

No. Soft materials (Al, Cu) clear scratches faster but smear and embed abrasive, so you save time on each step but may need extra final-polish work. Work-hardening alloys (stainless, Ni) need longer at coarser stages to avoid leaving a deformed surface layer. The estimator uses material-specific schedules under the hood.

Why does the result split "active" and "cure" time?

Cure time on castable mounts is passive; you're not at the bench. Splitting it lets you plan around it (start an epoxy mount before lunch or at end of day) rather than treating it as time at the lab bench.

How accurate is the estimate?

For routine metallography with well-maintained equipment, the ranges land within ±20% of real prep time. Outliers: very large specimens, heavily deformed surfaces, refractory materials (which can need substantially longer at every step), and labs running unfamiliar materials for the first time.

Should I always include the fine-grinding (800/1200) steps?

If you have time, yes: they make the transition into 9 μm polishing easier and reduce the time you spend at the first polishing step. If turnaround is critical and you're using a fresh 9 μm pad, you can usually skip directly from 600 grit to 9 μm with no loss of quality.

Can I run two materials on one automated holder?

It works but isn't ideal. The pressure, time, and consumable choice will be a compromise. A common workaround is to do the grinding pass material-by-material at the hand grinder, then group polished mounts on the automated holder for the finer steps where the recipes converge.
