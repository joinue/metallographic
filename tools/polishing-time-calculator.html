Polishing Time Calculator

Recommended polishing time, cloth, pressure, and notes for a single polishing step, based on PACE Technologies' 11-class preparation scheme. Pick a material class and an abrasive size; results update live.

Heads up: these times are for one polishing step in a sequence (e.g., 3 μm diamond after completing 9 μm and 6 μm). The sample should already be polished through the previous, coarser abrasive before this step.

Polishing Parameters

PACE 11 Preparation Classes

Don Zipperian's 11-class scheme groups materials by the properties that actually drive sample-prep choices: hardness, ductility, brittleness, and how the material responds to abrasion. Use the class to pick a procedure, not just the alloy name.

1
Soft, Ductile
Al, Cu, Pb, Sn, soft brass, precious metals
2
Refractory Metals
W, Mo, Ta, Nb, V
3
Lower Ductility Metals
Mg alloys, Zn alloys, some Al alloys
4
Soft Brittle Non-metals
Graphite, soft polymers, foams
5
Medium Hard / Ductile
Low-C steel, cast iron, bronze, soft tool steels
6
Tough, Hard Non-ferrous
Ti, Ni superalloys, austenitic / duplex stainless
7
Coatings & Surface Treatments
Thermal spray, hard chrome, nitride / PVD / CVD
8
Hardened Steels
Tool steels (D2, M2), bearing steels, Q&T
9
Metal Matrix Composites
SiC/Al, B₄C/Al, fiber-reinforced metals
10
Engineered Ceramics
Al₂O₃, SiC, Si₃N₄, ZrO₂
11
Very Hard Brittle Materials
Sintered carbides, CVD diamond, cermets, PCD/PCBN

How the Polishing Sequence Works

A polishing procedure is a sequence of decreasing abrasive sizes. Each step has a job: remove the scratches left by the previous, coarser step. If a step is skipped or cut short, the next step inherits the coarser damage and runs much longer, or never recovers.

  • Coarse → fine, always. Typical metals go 9 → 6 → 3 → 1 → 0.05 μm. Hard ceramics and carbides start at 15 μm and may include intermediate 0.25 / 0.5 μm steps.
  • Inspect between steps. Before moving to the next finer grit, the surface should show a uniform scratch pattern with no remnants from the previous step. Look at 100× or higher.
  • Rotate the sample 90° between steps. The new scratch direction makes leftover coarser scratches obvious, since they run perpendicular to the new ones.
  • One step's time depends on the prior step's quality. The numbers in this calculator assume the preceding step was completed properly. A rushed 9 μm step makes the 6 μm step run twice as long.

Pressure Guidelines

Pressure values in the results refer to the downward force applied to the sample on a manual polisher, or the equivalent platen pressure on an auto-polisher. Too much pressure causes deformation, smearing, and edge rounding; too little leaves coarse scratches uncleared.

Very Light
1 – 3 lbs

Soft / ductile materials and final polishing steps. Just enough weight to keep the sample flat on the cloth.

Light
2 – 5 lbs

Fine polishing on most metals and intermediate steps on hard materials.

Moderate
4 – 8 lbs

The workhorse range. Covers coarse polishing on steels, superalloys, and most metals.

High
8 – 12 lbs

Hard ceramics, sintered carbides, and other materials that resist removal at lower pressures.

Polishing Tips

  • Keep the cloth wet and the abrasive fresh. A drying cloth or depleted suspension stops cutting and starts smearing. That's the most common cause of an unexpectedly long polishing step.
  • Check the cloth for embedded coarser grit. Cross-contamination ruins fine steps. Dedicate a cloth to one abrasive size and protect them from each other.
  • Use the right diamond type. Polycrystalline diamond cuts faster (good for hard materials); monocrystalline gives a finer finish (better for soft / ductile materials).
  • Vibratory polish for hard-to-finish materials. Stainless, Ni superalloys, ceramics, and composites often benefit from a final vibratory step with colloidal silica. Far better surface than time-on-platen alone.
  • Don't chase a single bad scratch. Extending a fine step to remove one leftover coarse scratch causes relief everywhere else. Go back one step.
